About This Calculation

A real turbine-boiler-generator (BTG) unit is protected by dozens of overlapping systems, not one: the Emergency Trip System (ETS) protects the turbine-generator itself (overspeed, low vacuum, high vibration, thrust bearing failure), the Master Fuel Trip (MFT) protects the boiler (flame failure, low drum level, furnace pressure excursion), and dozens of individual auxiliary-drive interlocks and permissives sit underneath both. This registry models that structure directly — organized by ETS/MFT/auxiliary-drive category, each parameter tagged by data source (a defensible public-reference value, your own entered plant data, or a live simulated value) so it is always clear whether a number shown is a generic illustration or something you configured yourself.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the actual difference between an alarm, a permissive, an interlock, and a trip?

An alarm only notifies the operator — it takes no automatic action. A permissive is a condition that must already be true before an action is ALLOWED to proceed (e.g. a pump won’t start unless its discharge valve is open). An interlock actively PREVENTS or FORCES an action based on current conditions. A trip is the most severe: it automatically shuts down equipment (or the whole unit) because a protective limit was exceeded, and normally requires a deliberate reset before restart.

Why does the ETS exist separately from the plant DCS?

Overspeed, loss of vacuum, and similar turbine-generator protection functions need to act in milliseconds and must remain reliable even if the main control system has a fault — so ETS logic is traditionally implemented on dedicated, often hardwired or triple-redundant safety-rated hardware, kept deliberately independent of the DCS that handles normal day-to-day control.

Why does an MFT trip ALL fuel valves, not just reduce firing?

A furnace that has lost flame but continues receiving fuel builds up an unburned, explosive fuel-air mixture — re-ignition of that accumulated fuel is a genuine explosion hazard. Immediate, complete fuel isolation on any flame-failure or furnace-safety trip condition is a core NFPA 85 combustion-safety principle, not an operational choice.

Are the setpoints shown here real values from a specific OEM?

No — values tagged “Public Reference” are commonly-used generic figures for education and planning, explicitly not sourced from any specific manufacturer’s proprietary setpoint documents. Real trip setpoints for an actual unit must come from that unit’s own approved C&E (Cause & Effect) documentation.
